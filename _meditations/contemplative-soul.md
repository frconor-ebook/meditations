*By Fr. Conor Donnelly*

*(Proofread)*

*In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.*

*My Lord and my God, I firmly believe that you are here, that you see me, that you hear me. I adore you with profound reverence. I ask your pardon for my sins and grace to make this time of prayer fruitful. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me.*

We're told in Scripture that Our Lord says, "Lo, I am with you always, even unto the end of the world" (Matt. 28:20).

When speaking about "contemplating God in ordinary life" one may first think perhaps of contemplation "while" some activities are carried out: for instance, while walking down the street, or performing an action that doesn't require complete concentration.

This is not the central idea regarding contemplation in ordinary life, though it is an integral part of it. So St. Josemaría has a constant insistence in seeking to contemplate while carrying out other activities.

In one of his homilies, after speaking about seeking the *presence of God* throughout the day by means of various acts of piety, he says: "These pious practices will lead you, almost without your realizing it, to contemplative prayer. Your soul will pour forth more acts of love, aspirations, acts of thanksgiving, acts of atonement, spiritual communions.

"And this will happen while you go about your ordinary duties, when you answer the telephone, when you get on to a bus, when you open or close a door, when you pass in front of a church, when you begin a new task, during it, and when finish it: you will find yourself referring everything you do to your Father God" (Josemaría Escrivá, *Friends of God*, Point 149).

In this vein, contemplation can arise at the most unexpected moments and introduce us suddenly into divine intimacy. It was God's will for St. Josemaría to have an experience of this very early on with an extraordinary intensity, something which confirmed him in what he was then to transmit to many souls.

He wrote, "I can't understand it: I know of someone who is cold (in spite of his faith, which is unbounded) when he is close to the divine fire of the Tabernacle. And yet later, in the street, amid the noise of cars and trams and people---even while reading a newspaper!---he is carried away by a mad love for God" (J. Escrivá, *Apuntes íntimos*, Point 673, March 26, 1932).

The above notwithstanding, the central aspect of contemplation in the middle of the world is not this one, as we said before. Its essence lies even more deeply within ordinary activities.

Not only is it possible to contemplate God "while" carrying out a good activity, but also "through" that same activity and "in" it, even when full concentration of one's mind is required.

In one of his homilies, when talking about contemplation, he says, "The soul spends time lovingly with the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit" (J. Escrivá, *Friends of God*, Point 306).

And he adds that this contemplation is possible, "in my daily occupations, which are no hindrance to me; quite the contrary, they are my path, my reason to love more and more, and to be more and more united to my God" (*ibid*., Point 310).

And so, we find itemized, as it were, the most characteristic aspects of St. Josemaría's teaching on this topic.

We should see first of all that it is possible to contemplate God "through" ordinary activities given that, just as we see God through His works in which His glory is manifested, so too we can contemplate God "through" our own works as well, in the measure that they participate in His creative power.

To researchers and educators, he said: "Study and teaching---your professional work---are in our case a means of personal holiness, of union with God, of contemplative life: because," just as through the effects of God we can come to contemplate God Himself, according to St. Paul in his Letter to the Romans: 'The invisible attributes of God can be known through the things he has made' (Rom. 1:20), so, too, as a secondary element, says St. Thomas Aquinas, 'The contemplation of the divine effects belongs to the contemplative life, inasmuch as knowledge of these leads one to the knowledge of God' (Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, II-II, Q180)" (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 5*, Point 13, October 2, 1939).

The Christian contemplates God "through" a good activity, whatever it may be, when he sees how the divine perfections are reflected in the effects of that activity.

This happens, for example, in work carried out perfectly, or when he discovers God in something related to that activity, even if it is not an effect of that activity, as may happen when studying a natural phenomenon whereby the heart admires God's wisdom, goodness, and beauty.

It is also possible, he says, to contemplate God "in" the very task which is accomplished. "We recognize God not only in the wonders of nature, but also in the experience of our own work" (*cf*. J. Escrivá, *Christ Is Passing By*, Point 48).

Here we see what one author describes as a "bold anthropological path to reach God" (José Ignacio Murillo, *Trabajo y espíritu*).

A Christian can exercise contemplation "in" the very activity which freely springs from his heart, if he carries it out for love of God. When he works out of love, under the influence of the gifts of the Holy Spirit, he finds God "in the experience of his own work," because the love with which he carries it out is a participation in the Holy Spirit, who "searches everything, even the depths of God" (1 Cor. 2:10).

Someone who works for love of God can be conscious of the love with which he works and contemplate God there, in that love which the Paraclete places in his heart.

In some ways, this seems to be the core of St. Josemaría's teaching on contemplation. The three aspects are noted---contemplation "while" carrying out an activity, contemplation "through" that activity, and contemplation "in" that very activity---all these pertain to contemplation in ordinary life, but the last of these is the most profound.

Suffice it to consider the fact that a task can "turn out badly" despite the good will and best of intentions, and thus perhaps one may not perceive the reflection of the divine perfections in the effects of that work. Still, one can always contemplate God "in" the very activity that is being carried out, if it is done out of love for Him.

During His life in Nazareth, Jesus worked as a craftsman, just as St. Joseph did. We do not have any extant examples of His work, such as the furniture He made with the perfection the tools of His time permitted.

We do know about the love with which He accomplished that work. It's the same love which led Him to give His life for us on the Cross.

The Christian who carries out his obligations out of love for God and works in vital union with Christ turns that work in some way into the work of Christ.

St. Josemaría liked to say, "professional work, our human friendships and dealings with others, the effort to achieve the good---shoulder to shoulder with our fellow citizens---as well as the progress of society; these are...the natural fruit, the logical consequence of the life of Christ in our soul: they are the work of Christ," Opus Dei, *operatio Dei* (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 11*, Point 25, May 6, 1945).

For this, it is not sufficient to be in the grace of God and for the works to be morally good: they must be informed by love and accomplished in the divine way that proceeds from the gifts of the Holy Spirit.

Contemplation in ordinary life is a foretaste of the final end, the definitive union with God. It leads us to act every time with more love, with the desire of seeing God, no longer through the works themselves, but face to face:

"We live then as captives, as prisoners," he says. "While we carry out as perfectly as we can (with all our mistakes and limitations) the tasks allotted to us by our situation and duties, our soul longs to escape. It is drawn towards God like iron drawn by a magnet.

"One begins to love Jesus, in a more effective way, with the sweet and gentle surprise of his encounter. ... It is a new mode of going about this earth, a mode that is divine, supernatural, marvelous.

"In the style of some many Spanish writers of the sixteenth century, we may like to savor for ourselves this truth: 'I live because I do not live; it is Christ who lives in me! (*cf*. Gal. 2:20)' (J. Escrivá, *Friends of God*, Points 296-297).

Everyday human tasks are not our final end. The final end is the contemplation of God, and for that very reason, the contemplative soul "yearns to escape" to the definitive encounter with Him.

Especially in the last years of his life, St. Josemaría often repeated the words: *Vultum tuum, Domine, requiram!*--"Lord, I long to see your face! (Ps. 27:8). I have a great desire to see how the Lord is, yet no longer by faith, but face to face!" (J. Escrivá, *Notes*).

He gave the following explanation: "Those who are in love want to see each other. A lover has eyes only for the beloved" (*ibid*., December 25, 1973). But that desire is not incompatible with the affirmation that human tasks are a means to contemplate God if we carry them out as perfectly as we can.

St. Josemaría affirms that "a contemplative soul knows how to see Jesus Christ in those around him (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 3*, Point 70, January 9, 1932).

Contemplation in ordinary life is not an attitude closed off to others; on the contrary, it leads one to fuse the effort of personally giving glory to God with the desire of reflecting that glory so that all may glorify Him (see Matt. 5:16).

Contemplative life in the middle of the world demands transforming all activities into apostolic activity: "There are no isolated compartments in our lives," he said. "Neither should we be able to distinguish, I insist, where prayer ends and where work begins, nor where the limits of our apostolate are; because apostolate is the love of God, which overflows, giving itself to men; and contemplative interior life is a clamor for souls; and work, an effort sustained by self-denial, by charity, by obedience, by understanding, by patience, and by service to others" (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 11*, Point 40, May 6, 1945).

This is the horizon of Christian life that St. Josemaría opens up for us. To give glory to God is to be a contemplative in ordinary life.

"In our life, our life as Christians," he says, "our first conversion---that unique moment that each of us remembers, when we clearly understood everything the Lord is asking of us---is significant; but more important still, and increasingly demanding, are successive conversions" (J. Escrivá, *Christ Is* *Passing By*, Point 57).

The first conversion is the conscious and free decision to live for the glory of God instead of for oneself. This conversion must be strengthened and later reaffirmed by means of successive conversions, through specific choices---some will be important, though the majority of the time they will be small and ordinary---until one ends up doing "everything" for the glory of God.

In order to get to the core meaning of these successive conversions, it is very important to recognize that all our actions, at least implicitly, we have one final end as our intention. This end can only be God or "our own ego," as there are no other possibilities: we either love God and try to give Him glory in each of our acts, or we love ourselves more than God.

In this regard, St. Josemaría gives advice that can help us to live for the glory of God. He says: "It only takes a second. Before starting anything, ask yourself: 'What does God want of me in this?' Then, with divine grace, do it!" (J. Escrivá, *The Way*, Point 778).

In addition to being well-disposed to fulfill God's will and give Him glory, St. Josemaría asked God to show him specifically what He wished from him: "Lord, that I may see!" (Luke 18:41).

Here we should mention the temptation of being content with doing good things, but without asking oneself if these are what God is really asking us to do.

It can happen that we choose an apparent good, something which in theory is good but not what God wishes us to do here and now. It is not sufficient for something to be good "in itself" for it to be good "for me." Holiness does not consist in "doing good things" but in precisely fulfilling the will of God for each one.

For this reason, St. Josemaría notes that "in choosing to seek sanctity in the place where God has put us, we have to leave aside other good things, but that now those things are not our path" (J. Escrivá, *Notes*, June 21, 1972).

"We have to humbly ask God," he says, "to grant us this wisdom so that we may learn to see every event with this perspective" (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 38*, Point 143, March 19, 1967).

God's glory is not only the ultimate end of all the actions of the Christian; it must be so totally and exclusively: "Give 'all' the glory to God. With your will aided by grace, 'squeeze' out each one of your actions, so that nothing remains in them that smacks of human pride, of self-complacency" (J. Escrivá, *The Way*, Point 784).

In our situation, due to the consequences of original sin and personal sins, even when someone wishes to live for God's glory, the attention of the will is easily trusted in concrete choices. That is why St. Josemaría indicates the need to reset one's intention time and time again: "To rectify," he says. "A little each day. This must be your constant concern if you really want to become a saint" (J. Escrivá, *ibid*., Point 290).

When carrying out actions that form part of our duties, good and holy intentions may be mixed with others that are not upright. It is therefore not to simply conform ourselves to carrying out externally what is expected.

This is what St. Josemaría often refers to as *purity of intention*. "The insinuation of pride and the impulses of the flesh are easy to recognize...and you fight and, with grace, you conquer," he says.

"But the motives that inspire you, even in your holiest actions, don't seem clear. ... And deep down inside you hear a voice which makes you aware of your human motives, so that your soul is subtly daunted by the disturbing thought that you are not acting as you should, for pure love, solely and exclusively to give God all his glory."

And so, he says, "React at once each time and say: 'Lord, for myself I want nothing. All for your glory and for love'" (*ibid*., Point 788).

Any end which a Christian sets for himself in professional, family, or other matters, should be ordered to the ultimate end: the glory of God.

In order to accomplish this, it is necessary to make the effort with God's grace to always connect the immediate ends with the last end. In other words, in the good things that a person wishes to accomplish, he should not lose sight of the fact that the goal is holiness in order to evaluate them under this light.

Referring, for instance, to apostolic initiatives, he says: "I measure the effectiveness of the apostolic works by the level of sanctity attained by those who carry them out. ... Universities, residences, a home management school. ... These are aims? Just as the spade and hoe are not the aim of the farmer, but rather the means to work the earth" (J. Escrivá, *Notes*).

By consciously allowing for an immediate end that is "disconnected" from holiness, regardless of how noble it may be, one would be misled from the glory of God.

It would not make sense either to neglect holiness, "forgetting God for a moment," or to consciously "put him in parenthesis" when faced with the urgent need of fulfilling an obligation, or of carrying out some task, or of resting. Any good action that is not for the glory of God is only apparently good. It does not place one on the path of Christian holiness.

One would have to be able to do without this good, no matter how important it is. We're told in St. Matthew, "For what will it profit a man, if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" (Matt. 16:26).

If a good, considered in an abstract manner, is a danger for losing one's path towards holiness, it cannot be a true good and it should elicit a proper reaction: "We have all heard stories about ships laden with precious cargo that are saved from sinking because the crew cast all the treasure and bullion overboard.

"If one day it happens," says St. Josemaría, "it doesn't have to happen, and it won't if you are perceptive and humble---that you begin to see a conflict in your work between freedom and vocation, it's the moment to follow the sailors' example and throw everything that gets in the way overboard, saying to Our Lord, 'Here I am because you have called me' (1 Sam. 3:6)" (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 14*, Point 8, October 15, 1948).

Vainglory, or seeking one's own personal glory, which is a form of idolatry, is the opposite of God's glory. "Our Lord wants us to be humble. But that humility doesn't mean that you fail to reach the standing you should in your profession or in your ordinary work, and of course in your spiritual life.

"It is important to succeed, but without seeking oneself, with an upright intention. We do not live for this life only, nor for our own glory. Rather we live for the glory of God, for the service of God: this alone moves us" (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 1*, Point 21, March 24, 1930).

If giving glory to God means contemplating Him, vainglory is, in some sense, "contemplating" oneself and seeking to be contemplated by others; it means loving one's own excellence whether real or imagined for itself, and seeking others to recognize and admire it (John 12:43).

Self-love includes as its primary element thinking about oneself. Instead, love for God necessarily leads one to think about God and others, seek God's presence, and be concerned for the good of others, forgetting oneself the point that it is possible to be able to say sincerely when we do the examination of conscience at night:

"Lord, today I haven't thought about myself at all! I've been thinking only about you, and for your sake I've been concerned only about working for others!" (J. Escrivá, *Notes*).

The conviction that God calls all to be contemplatives should lead us to struggle to remove all the obstacles that prevent us from receiving that gift.

In the end, selfishness and its multiple manifestations is the obstacle. Among them is our "interior monologue," that is, our unrestrained imagination and memory, disordered affections, unchecked senses, and so forth. We must seek a constant dealing with God, freely, freely cooperating with grace:

"We have to dedicate all our efforts, night and day, to uniting our soul and our spirit to Our Father God through prayer, through a ceaseless loving contemplation.

"When your senses, your imagination, the potencies of the soul, are all immersed in God, then you won't have any personal problems, and being divinized, you will be able to say, as St. Paul says to the Galatians, 'It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me' (Gal. 2:20).

"You will require an unquenchable hunger, a thirst for God, and you will experience in your own life the truth of those words that are contained in the Book of Sirach: 'Those who eat me will hunger for more, and those who drink me will thirst for more' (Sir. 24:21)" (J. Escrivá, *Collected Letters*: *Letter 11*, Point 28, May 6, 1945).

As we think about Our Lady, and "kept all these words carefully in her heart" (Luke 2:19), whose gaze was continually on the face of her Son, we could ask Our Lady to lead us forward day by day in that pathway to being a true contemplative in the middle of the world.

*I thank you, my God, for the good resolutions, affections, and inspirations that you have communicated to me during this meditation. I ask your help to put them into practice. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me.*

*In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.*

*EW*
