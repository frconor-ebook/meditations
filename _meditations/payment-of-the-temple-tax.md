By Fr. Conor Donnelly

*(Proofread)*

*In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen. My Lord and my God, I firmly believe that You are here, that You see me, that You hear me. I adore You with profound reverence. I ask Your pardon for my sins and grace to make this time of prayer fruitful. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me.*

"When they reached Capernaum, the collectors of the half-shekel came to Peter and said, 'Does your master not pay the half-shekel?' 'Yes,' he replied, and went into the house. But before he could speak, Jesus said, 'Simon, what is your opinion? From whom do earthly kings take toll or tribute? From their sons, or from foreigners?' And when he replied, 'From foreigners,' Jesus said, 'Well then, the sons are exempt. However, so that we shall not be the downfall of others, go to the lake and cast a hook; take the first fish that rises, open its mouth, and there you will find a shekel. Take it and give it to them for me and for yourself'" (Matt. 17:24--27).

Our Lord is telling us here that to be good Christians, we have to be exemplary citizens. It's part of our Christian vocation. The natural is the natural and the supernatural is the supernatural. And the natural can be the foundation or the basis or the pathway to the supernatural.

They had just arrived in Capernaum, and the tithe collectors of the temple approached Peter asking, "Will your master not pay the didrachma?" (Matt. 17:24). Every Jew over the age of 20 was required to pay an annual sum of two drachmas to support the worship of God in the temple of Jerusalem. This was an obligation for all, even the Jews not living in Palestine. And Peter's affirmative answer, given without consulting Jesus, suggests that Our Lord usually paid this tax.

The conversation probably took place outside the house. Our Lord was not close by at the time. So when Peter entered the house, Jesus, already inside, asked, "What do you think, Simon? From whom do the kings of the earth receive tribute or custom? From their own sons or from others?" (Matt. 17:25).

Under the ancient monarchies, the tribute was considered a special payment for the benefit of the royal family. Hence, Our Lord's question to Peter, "From whom do the kings of the earth receive tribute?"

The answer was easy. From their subjects, from the others. The sons are exempt. With respect to the payment of the tribute, Jesus is in the same position as the sons of the king. By declaring himself exempt, he also teaches us that he is God's own Son and he lives in the Father's house. So he's not obliged to pay the tribute. But Our Lord wanted to fulfill his duties as a citizen, as other men did. However, he made his divinity clear to all in the way that he obtained the payment, the money for the payment.

This gospel account, recorded only by Saint Matthew, also shows us Our Lord's spirit of poverty and detachment, where he lacked even two drachmas. A small sum even in those days. Significant also is that Our Lord paid Peter's share along with his own.

"But that we may not give offense to them," said Jesus to Peter, "go down to the sea and cast a hook, and take the first fish that comes up. And opening its mouth, you will find a stater. Take that and give it to them for me and for you" (Matt. 17:27). The stater equaled the sum of four drachmas.

Saint Ambrose comments, "This is a great lesson that teaches Christians to be submissive to the temple authority, so that no one should disobey the edicts of the kings of the earth."

Saint Josemaría, in one of the chapters in *The Forge* on work, talks about the good example that we're all called to give in being good citizens. He said, "Christian, you have the obligation of being an example in everything you do, including being an example as a citizen in your fulfillment of the laws directed to the common good."

Well, these are very basic lessons that we can teach our children. Not to throw sweet papers on the floor or banana skins out the window of a bus or plastic bottles or any place that we may go, into a park, to a beach, where we have a picnic or a mountain, to try and leave the place a little better than we found it, so that anybody else who comes along might find that place in a better way than we did.

In this way we care for the environment, foster a spirit of order, of cleanliness and hygiene. Same thing in relation to public washrooms, everything that doesn't belong to us, we have to take care of it as if we did. Have some concern for public property, take care of it, make it last, try and keep it in good shape.

In another point he says, "Carry out all your duties as a citizen, do not try to get out of any of your obligations. Exercise all your rights too, for the good of society without making any rash exceptions. You must give Christian witness in that also."

Our Lord is talking about how we live our Christian vocation in all situations. We try to live virtue in all situations.

I heard of a man once who was bringing his children into a sort of a fun park, and he asked the person at the door, at the gate, "Well, how much was it for entrance?" And this person said, "Well, for adults it's such and such, and for children it's such and such, but children under seven are free." So the man paid for his two children, one was eight and the other was nine. And the person at the gate said, "You know, mister, if you told me that your children were under seven, I wouldn't have known the difference and you would have got in free." And the man said, "Yes," he said, "but my children would have seen that I was telling a lie."

So honesty in all situations, fulfilling our duties as citizens, also to educate our children and our grandchildren to be good citizens, to live by what is right, to have a concern for the common good.

The common good is a very Christian concept which basically means concern for the well-being of every single person in society. Some people like to talk about the greatest good for the greatest majority. But if you follow that principle, what happens to the minorities? The concept of the common good is very Christian, respects the dignity of all persons. Doesn't matter where they're from, or what they look like, or how much money they have, or what work they do. That person has dignity. And if that person has a problem, well then as Christians we have to try and help them to solve that problem. Even if they're a stranger, who's had a problem on the road, or an accident, or they're not well. We don't walk by on the other side of the road.

If the Son of God paid the tribute, well, we can ask ourselves, are we great enough to refuse payment? And even he who possessed nothing paid the tribute. Saint Ambrose says, "You who seek the riches of the earth, why do you not recognize the duties which go along with these goods? Why do you consider yourself above the world?"

So our Christian vocation and our Christian formation helps us to see that all the things that God has given to us are a gift. And we have to try and spread those riches, those goods around as much as possible. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* talks about the universal destination of the goods of the earth. The goods of the earth are meant to reach all people.

And all of that virtue has to start at home, paying a proper salary to people who work with us, being concerned about the welfare of our workers, about their families, their living conditions, their food, their shelter, their clothing, their rest.

From this and other passages, we see that to learn in order to imitate Our Lord, to be good citizens who fulfill their duties at work, in the family, and in society. First and foremost, that can mean while we're on time for our work. And we finish our work well. We do a good day's work for a good day's pay. And while we're at work, we do our work. We're told in Saint Matthew that Our Lord saw Matthew sitting at work in the tax collector's place. He was where he should have been. He wasn't down the road playing billiards, or taking coffee. He was sitting at work. He was doing what he should have been doing. He was fulfilling his duty as a citizen.

All these obligations then include paying fair taxes, voting according to our conscience, and participating in public services are part of all these duties. We're told in *Furrow*, "Love and respect the ways of behaving by which you may live in amity with other people. Have no doubt either, that your loyal submission to duty can be the means for others to discover Christian integrity, which is the fruit of divine love, and to find God."

Often a Christian father or mother gives a great example by just being a good father or mother, being there for their children, controlling them in public places, or looking out for them. I had the experience recently in an airport departure lounge of seeing a fairly young couple with three fairly young children. They had to wait a long time for the next plane, and the kids were running around the place a little bit. One of the kids looked about one or two. But it was very interesting just to watch the parents, how they functioned, not doing anything unusual, being very ordinary, but obviously they were concerned for the well-being of their children. On the one hand, they weren't letting them run all over the place. And on the other, they were very affectionate, trying to help the children to bear this delay that there was. And I had the privilege of telling those parents a little while later. They told me they understood English, and I was able to tell them, "Well, you have a beautiful family." Very nice thing to be able to tell people.

Thus, the example of our life in the middle of the world is also very powerful. After the descent of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, the apostles were clearly aware they'd been sent out by the Lord to make him present in the midst of society. We're told in Saint John that like the master, they were not of the world. The world would often reject them. Seldom would they enjoy the benevolent smile that men reserve for something that is their own. Often they were outcasts, not listened to, not understood. Without being of the world, without being worldly, the first Christians rejected customs and behavior that were incompatible with the faith they had received, although they never felt out of place in the society to which they rightly belonged.

So they had a clear criteria. There were certain places they didn't go to, certain things they didn't do. There are certain types of behavior that they didn't accept in their own family. We are family and we don't do those sort of things in this family. Sort of things that you might tell your children. We live in a certain way, we're home at a certain hour, we're present for certain meal times. There are certain family gatherings that we make it a point to be present for; these are family values or family priorities.

And in the preaching of the apostles, they put a special emphasis on their place in the very heart of society. The vocation of ordinary laypeople in the middle of the world, emphasized by the Second Vatican Council, was about a certain revolution in the Church. The evangelization that has to take place through our example, through our lifestyle, also through our words, through the witness that we give.

It's there in the middle of the world that they would exercise their influence fully. Salt is for seasoning and for preserving from corruption the lives of men. Yeast is united completely with the flour, and thus acts to ferment and raise the entire mass. Their light should shine before men, so that others, convinced through the deeds they have seen, may glorify the Father in heaven. Our Lord is banking a lot on our Christian witness as ordinary citizens in the middle of the world, exemplary, honorable people, with whom it's a pleasure to have anything to do, because we practice the virtues. We can be relied upon, trusted.

The first Christians didn't seek isolation, nor did they put up barriers to hide behind and to fell themselves in times of persecution. Their attitude during trials was neither pugnacious nor faint-hearted. They were serene.

And like yeast, unapparent, within the mass of society. And that's the sort of role that God has called each one of us to play in a very ordinary way, fulfilling our ordinary duties in the family, in the place, in the job that we have been given.

Consequently, the Christian presence in the world was a radical affirmation, and all the fury of the persecutors was incapable of shaking the serene and positive attitude of the Christians. They were exemplary citizens. The violence of the persecutions did not make them either maladjusted or antisocial people, nor did it change their basic attitude of solidarity with other men, their peers.

Tertullian said, "We are accused of separating ourselves from the popular masses of the state, which is false. Because the Christian knows that he is in the same boat as his fellow citizens, shipmates in a common earthly destiny. If the empire is shaken by violence, this evil affects all her subjects, ourselves included."

Even though there were times they were slandered and misunderstood, the Christians remained faithful to their divine vocation and to their human vocation. They occupied their proper position in the world. They didn't flee from the world. They exercised their rights, carefully fulfilled their duties.

When Paul was persecuted in Rome, he pointed out that, "I am a Roman citizen." Humility also means that we exercise our lawful rights and call on them, that they be respected.

The first Christians were not just good Christians. They didn't just hide in sacristies. The role of the layman is not just to be the server of the priest or a clerical mentality or talking about all sorts of clerical things like the latest news from the Episcopal conference. We have to know how to talk about the Premier League or the hockey league or the volleyball league or politics, or whatever other things that other people are talking about. Because we're not like other people in the world. We are the other people in the world.

So they were exemplary citizens because their duties as citizens were the obligations of a well-formed conscience. In fulfilling those duties, they sanctified themselves. They obeyed just civil laws not only out of fear of punishment, but also as a matter of conscience. It was the right thing to do. It was the honest thing to do.

Saint Paul says to the Christians of Rome, pay the tribute. Saint Justin says, "As we learned from him, from Christ, we pay the tribute and contributions fully and promptly. Thus though we adore only God, we gladly obey you and all the rest, fully acknowledging that you are the kings and governors of men, and praying that along with the imperial power, you also possess a skill in governing that is full of wisdom."

So in our prayer today, we could ask ourselves, Am I known as a good citizen, who punctually fulfills their duties? Am I a good neighbor, a good colleague to my fellow workers? Do I try and encourage my children to, well, to do good deeds for perhaps some elderly neighbors, help them in household chores or other things, be of service to them, be attentive to their needs? Keep an eye out for some difficult moments they might be passing through.

And this duty as citizens also means being present whenever the good of society is decided. The Church has always encouraged Christians, citizens of the temporal city and of the eternal city, to faithfully fulfill their temporal duties, guided always by the spirit of the gospel. *Gaudium et spes* from the Second Vatican Council, the Church in the Modern World, speaks very clearly about this.

Other people should see in us the light of Christ reflected in our honest work, in the good service that we give. And very often, the goodness of the service or the effectiveness of the service involves double-checking. Is this client happy? Is the service that has been rendered appropriate? Has the job been well done? Organizations and people that look for feedback, generally, it's a very good sign of professionalism. They want to improve, they want to do a better job, they want to be sure that it's a better job objectively speaking, not just what they subjectively think may be a good job.

Thus, we have to try and fulfill our due obligations to our employer and fellow employees, and also to society through the payment of just taxes.

Saint Josemaría liked to say that one of the important, the most important duties of students is to study, to know their stuff well, so that they can implement it well later on, be a better professional person. They have to try and strive to form their conscience properly in their new profession. Professors should prepare their classes daily, improving their lessons year after year, without falling into routine or mediocrity.

Mothers should take care of their home, or fathers also, if that's their role, taking care of their children, their spouse, being a fair sum to whatever household manager that may be there, always trying to fulfill their duties with perfection.

So the Christian cannot be satisfied with fulfilling only their family and religious duties. Saint Josemaría liked to say that a woman or a man who spends all day in church but doesn't prepare the meals well for their children or burns the toast, well, they're not a saint. They could be a devil.

A Christian has to be present, if at all possible, where the affairs of the neighborhood or the town or the city or the country are decided, have an influence, make a splash. That's part of our Christian vocation. Our life has a social and political dimension that springs from our faith and that affects the exercise of the virtues, the essence of Christian life.

From this perspective, the social and political dimension of charity acquires its full nobility and dignity. It's an effect of love for other persons that's realized in the pursuit of the common good of society.

The Church has documents about the role of a Christian politician in the middle of the world. Saint Thomas More is the patron saint of politicians. Gives us a very good example. Not everybody can get involved in public life, but those that can, should. And everybody else can influence public life by making sure that public representatives know how you think and how you feel on certain issues.

As Christians, we're called to be saints in the middle of the world. We have to try and keep present the nobility and moral dignity of social and political commitments, and the great opportunities that they offer to grow in faith, in hope, in love, and also in fortitude, in detachment and generosity.

And those social and political commitments are lived with the Christian spirit, they become a strenuous school of perfection, a demanding means of living the virtues. Many years ago, there was a candidate called Geraldine Ferraro, I think in San Francisco, who was planning to run for the presidency of the United States in the mid-80s, and she said she was Catholic. But she said, "When I go into the political arena, I can't impose my views on other people. So, while I'm personally against abortion, in the public arena, I might have to go along with the laws on abortion." And the Cardinal Archbishop of New York came out very quickly and said, "That's not valid." You want to be a Catholic, you're a Catholic 24 hours, 24/7, through and through. You can't take off your Catholic hat when you go into the public arena.

We live a unity of life. We live our values and our virtues and our faith in all situations. These ideas are very applicable in many other places. And if we're citizens who fulfill our duties in an exemplary way, well, that's the way that we show the pathway to Christ to many other people. There might be an awful lot of people who don't know us personally, with whom we'll never have personal dealings, we won't talk to them, but they might get to know of our example or they might read some letter we place in the newspaper. Or they might see us at a distance helping some less privileged person in society. And maybe the Holy Spirit will work through that.

In many countries of formerly Christian tradition, well, a new and unformed mass of people has arisen. And while the world in all its breadth is a field of apostolic action that to reach all men, the task to which all Christians are committed today, the Church and all of her children are pretty much enrolled in this mission. The 11 should now exercise her renewing function, renew those countries of former Christian tradition. And that becomes all the more possible when we realize that we are full-fledged citizens, with rights to exercise, and duties to fulfill, and with a desire to face up to the difficulties that life in the middle of the world may entail.

Notice how the angel said to Saint Joseph to "flee into Egypt, and remain there until I tell you" (Matt. 2:13). There were a lot of challenges, possibly, in that remaining there: new country, new language, new culture, new places, no employment, refugee in that place. But yet called to live the Christian vocation to the full in that new environment. So we can ask Our Lady that she might help us to go a little deeper in this whole area of our social responsibility, so that we can be exemplary citizens, and follow the example of Joseph and Mary and Our Lord in fulfilling very well all the laws of the country in which we live.

*I thank You, my God, for the good resolutions, affections, and inspirations that You have communicated to me during this meditation. I ask Your help to put them into practice. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me. In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.*
