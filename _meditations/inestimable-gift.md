By Fr. Conor Donnelly

*(Proofread)*

*In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen. My Lord and my God, I firmly believe that you are here, that you see me, that you hear me. I adore you with profound reverence. I ask your pardon for my sins, and grace to make this time of prayer fruitful. My immaculate Mother, St. Joseph, my father and Lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me.*

In 2003, St. John Paul II published his encyclical on the relationship between the church and the Eucharist, in which he said that the Eucharist is at the center of the mystery of the church.

He said this is summarized in the Vatican II decree on the ministry and life of priests, where it says, "for in the most blessed Eucharist is contained the church's entire spiritual wealth: Christ himself, our Passover and living bread." Through his own flesh, now made living and life-giving by the Holy Spirit, he offers life to men.

At every celebration of the Eucharist, we are spiritually brought back to the Paschal Triduum, to the events of the evening of Holy Thursday, to the last supper and what followed it. Or John Paul says, the church's foundation and wellspring is the whole Triduum paschale. But this is, as it were, gathered up, foreshadowed, and concentrated forever in the gift of the Eucharist.

In this gift, Jesus Christ entrusts to his church the perennial making present of the Paschal mystery. With it, he brought about a mysterious oneness in time between the Triduum and the passage of the centuries.

The thought of what the Eucharist is fills St. John Paul II with amazement and wonder. He says that the congregation should also be filled with amazement as it reflects on this great sacrament.

But in a special way, the consideration of the Eucharistic mystery should fill the heart and mind of the priest, as he reflects on the extraordinary power that God has placed in his hands at ordination.

In his letter, *At the Beginning of the New Millennium*, John Paul asks everyone to contemplate the face of Christ, so as to be able to recognize him whenever he manifests his presence.

He said the church draws her life from Christ in the Eucharist, by him she is fed, by him she is enlightened. Whenever the church celebrates the Eucharist, the faithful can in some way relive the experience of the two disciples on the road to Emmaus. "Their eyes were opened, and they recognized him" (Luke 24:31).

As spiritual food of the faithful, the Eucharist, St. John Paul says, is the most precious possession the church can have on her journey through history. This is why the church has always emphasized the greatness of the Eucharistic mystery and the reality of eating and drinking Christ's body and blood.

While he outlines the progress in Eucharistic devotion in the church since the Second Vatican Council, he also, with not a little disappointment, draws attention to the dark clouds of unacceptable doctrine and practice which have appeared.

He points out how in some places, the practice of Eucharistic devotion has almost been completely abandoned.

At times, he says, one encounters an extremely reductive understanding of the Eucharistic mystery, stripped of its sacrificial meaning, and celebrated as if it were a simple fraternal banquet.

Furthermore, the necessity of the ministerial priesthood, grounded in apostolic succession, is at times obscured, and the sacramental nature of the Eucharist is reduced to its mere effectiveness as a form of proclamation.

"How can we not express profound grief," he says, "at all of this? The Eucharist is too great a gift to tolerate ambiguity and depreciation."

The Pope said that his hope was that the document, *Ecclesia de Eucharistia*, the Church of the Eucharist, would effectively help banish the dark clouds of unacceptable doctrine and practice, so that the Eucharist would continue to shine in all its radiant mystery.

But he didn't consider, obviously, the encyclical sufficiently detailed to adequately address the Eucharistic abuses which he outlined in some parts of that encyclical. Within a year of its publication, he felt it necessary to authorize the issuance of a detailed instruction that would not only remove aberrations, but would also enhance all ceremonies in Eucharistic practice.

This was the instruction, *Redemptionis Sacramentum*, published in 2004. It reminds us that the Eucharist in this is contained the whole spiritual wealth of the church, namely, Christ our Paschal Lamb, and states that the purpose of this instruction is to safeguard the mystery, the great mystery of the Eucharist.

He says it is not possible to remain silent about the abuses, even quite grave ones, against the nature of the liturgy and the sacraments, as well as the tradition and authority of the church, which in our day, do not infrequently plague liturgical celebrations in one ecclesial environment or another.

In some cases, the perpetration of liturgical abuses have become almost habitual. He says that the external observance of the liturgical norms published by the church is not enough. Remedial action must therefore be illumined by faith and charity, which unites us with Christ and teaches us to think as he himself does.

He reiterates the point that he made in his encyclical, that abuses contribute to the obscuring of the Catholic faith and doctrine concerning this wonderful sacrament.

You can see from his writings that the liturgical abuse was a permanent concern during the whole of his pontificate.

One of the consequences of our receiving the Blessed Eucharist, John Paul affirms, is that it spurs us on to make good use of our time and plants a living hope in our daily commitment to work.

While the Eucharist makes us look to the future, this increases, rather than lessens, our sense of responsibility for the world today. It is in this world, St. John Paul says, that the Christian hope must shine forth.

For this reason, too, Our Lord wished to remain with us in the Eucharist, making his presence in meal and sacrifice the promise of a humanity renewed by love.

All who take part in the Eucharist should be committed to changing their lives and making them, in a certain way, completely Eucharistic. It is the fruit of a transfigured existence, and a commitment to transforming the world in accordance with the gospel that splendidly illustrates the eschatological tension inherent in the celebration of the Eucharist and in the Christian life as a whole.

This conviction of the transforming presence of the laity in the world, imbued with the Eucharistic spirit, is one that John Paul II articulated many times during his pontificate. Because the Eucharist builds the church, and the church constructs the Eucharist, it follows that there's a profound relationship between the two.

In the way that the church is one, holy, catholic, and apostolic, so, in an analogous way, the Eucharist has also each of these four characteristics. Like the church, the Eucharist has its foundation on the apostles in that it was entrusted by Jesus to the apostles. In continuity with the practice of the apostles, and in obedience to the Lord's command, the church has celebrated the Eucharist through the centuries.

The Eucharist is also apostolic in that it is celebrated in conformity with the faith of the apostles, with the words they heard from Our Lord's lips. The Eucharist is also apostolic in another sense, only the priest has the power to consecrate the body and blood of Christ.

The priest, says John Paul, offers the Eucharistic sacrifice in persona Christi, in the person of Christ, in the specific sacramental identification with the eternal high priest, who is the author and principal subject of this sacrifice. A sacrifice which in truth, nobody can take his place.

The power of the priest is to offer this sacrifice. It infinitely transcends the power of the assembly. An ordained minister is essential to bring about the Eucharistic consecration, so that no representative of the assembly can substitute for the priest.

The minister is a gift that the assembly receives through episcopal succession going back to the apostles.

John Paul liked to say that it is in prayer before the Blessed Sacrament that the most effective winning of vocations for the priesthood takes place. This is a devotion that should be encouraged, he said, among the lay faithful, helping them to give effect to those words of Christ, "that the harvest is great, but the laborers are few. Ask the Lord of the harvest to send more laborers into the harvest" (Matt. 9:37--38).

Often it is the example of a priest's fervent pastoral charity, which Our Lord uses to sow and to bring to fruition in a young man's heart the seeds of a priestly calling. The lack of priests should cause the community to pray with more fervor to the Lord to send more vocations.

John Paul II speaks with great sadness about the Christian communities who do not have a priest to celebrate the Eucharist and the other sacraments. Their situation, he said, should bring them to implore God to send them priestly vocations.

I think it is probable to say that John Paul II, by dint of his priestly example, his outgoing, attractive personality, and his devotion to the Eucharist, he stirred up more vocations in the church during his pontificate than any other individual has. Certainly, vocations worldwide started to increase with his papacy.

An episode that serves as a prelude to the institution of the Eucharist, is the anointing at Bethany. Mary, the sister of Lazarus, pours a flask of costly ointment over Our Lord's head. And Jesus sees this act of anointing as an anticipation of the honor that his body will continue to merit, even after his death.

Like Mary of Bethany, in using the expensive ointment, the church does not fear extravagance in devoting her best resources to express her wonder and adoration at the unsurpassable gift of the Eucharist.

The church's awareness of which resources are worthy to use to celebrate the Eucharist has been refined over the centuries by the growth and appreciation of the ineffable gift of the Eucharist. In the wake of Our Lord's own words and actions, and building upon the ritual heritage of Jerusalem, the Christian liturgy was born.

John Paul II develops the relationship between art and the Eucharist, and in this sense prepares the ground for many things that Pope Benedict would say in subsequent years on this topic.

The historical development of devotion to the Eucharist has, says St. John Paul, led to an ecclesial traditions that emphasize the grandeur of the event being celebrated. On this foundation, a rich artistic heritage has discovered and developed.

Architecture, sculpture, painting, stained glass, and music have found in the Eucharist, both directly and indirectly, a source of great inspiration. It can be said that the Eucharist while shaping the church and her spirituality, has also powerfully affected culture, and the arts in particular.

Church art in the East, the result of the Greco-Byzantine tradition, has, John Paul II affirms, preserved a remarkably powerful sense of mystery, which led artists to see their efforts as creating beauty, not simply as an expression of their own talents, but as a genuine service to the faith.

"Adoro te devote," in Latin: "I devoutly adore you, O hidden God," who devoutly adore you, is the first line of the famous Eucharistic hymn attributed to St. Thomas Aquinas.

Jesus promised, "I will remain with you always," but he does so hidden behind the disguise of transubstantiated bread. We see Christ's humility in this hiddenness. It was part of God's plan for Jesus, like every child, to remain hidden in his mother's womb for nine months.

As he grew up in Nazareth and worked there as a carpenter, his divinity lay hidden, until at the age of 30, he began his public life of preaching and healing. And as St. Josemaría says, the humility of Jesus, in Bethlehem, in Nazareth, on Calvary. But still more humiliation and more self-abasement in the most sacred host, more than in the stable, more than in Nazareth, more than on the cross.

In the Blessed Eucharist, our senses are unable to perceive the real presence. We look at Jesus present in the tabernacle, perhaps just a few yards away, and we tell him that we know through faith that he is present.

The same Jesus who was born in Bethlehem and grew up in Nazareth. The same Jesus who rose from the dead, ascended into heaven, and is now seated at the right hand of the Father.

St. John describes a very moving scene after the resurrection, when Thomas, after refusing to believe the testimony of the other apostles, is confronted by the risen Christ, who elicits a deep act of faith from Thomas.

He said to him, "Put your finger here, and see my hands, and put your hand, and place it in my side. Do not be faithless, but believing" (John 20:27). Thomas responded immediately, "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28), expressing his faith in the risen one.

Our Lord replies, "Have you believed because you've seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet believe" (John 20:29).

Our Lord uses the occasion to emphasize that faith is a virtue that allows us to accept a truth that is a mystery, and that this is a blessed thing to do. He wants our faith to be made manifest before the tabernacle, in our thoughts and words.

St. Josemaría says, "Build a gigantic faith in the Holy Eucharist. Be filled with wonder before this ineffable reality." We have God with us. We can receive him every day. And if we want to, we can speak intimately with him just as we talk with a friend, as we talk with a brother, as we talk with the Father, as we talk with love itself.

St. Teresa of Avila wrote that when she heard the people say they wished they had lived when Christ walked on the earth, she would smile to herself, for she knew that we have him as truly with us in the Blessed Sacrament as people had him then. And what more could they possibly want?

And the holy of ours points out that our fortune is even greater than that of the people who lived with Christ during his life on earth. Because they sometimes had to walk for hours or days to find him, while we have him so close to us in every tabernacle.

He is there, we are told in the book of Psalms, as though behind a wall. And from there, he looks at us, as though from behind the lattice.

Even though we cannot see him, he looks at us from that place where he is truly present, so that we may possess him, even though he conceals himself in order that we may seek him out.

Until such time as we reach our celestial home, Our Lord wants to surrender himself entirely to us, and to live united to us in this way.

Everything in this world will come to an end, except the presence of Christ.

Ronald Knox describes it, all the din and clatter of the streets, all the great factories which dominate our landscape, are only echoes and shadows, if you think of them for a moment in the light of eternity.

The reality is in here, is there above the altar, is that part of it which our eyes cannot see and our senses cannot distinguish.

When death brings us into another world, the experience will not be that of one who falls asleep and dreams, but that of one who wakes from a dream into the full light of day.

Here, we are so surrounded by the things of sense, that we take them for the full reality. Only sometimes we have a glimpse which corrects that wrong perspective. And above all, when we see the Blessed Sacrament enthroned, we should look up towards that white disc which shines in the monstrance, as towards a chink through which just for a moment, the light of the other world shines through.

It's not surprising that St. John Paul would finish off his encyclical with words of deep affection for Our Lady in relation to the Eucharist, coupled with a penetrating theological analysis of her role in this context.

"Our Lady can guide us towards the Eucharist," he says, "as she herself had a profound relationship with it. In a certain sense, she lived a Eucharistic faith, even before the institution of the Eucharist, when she carried Jesus in her womb."

Mary became, in a way, the first tabernacle for her son. As she traveled from Nazareth to the hill country in Judea to visit her cousin Elizabeth. For the first nine months of her son's existence, Mary lived physically very close to her son, awaiting with deep expectation the day she would be able to contemplate his face.

We can learn from Our Lady, said Pope John Paul, how to express our love for Jesus and our thanksgiving after communion.

The Eucharist, while commemorating the passion and resurrection, is also in continuity with the incarnation.

At the Annunciation, Mary conceived God the Son in the physical reality of his body and blood, thus anticipating within herself what to some degree happens sacramentally in every believer who receives under the signs of bread and wine Our Lord's body and blood.

We can be sure that Mary participated in the Eucharist, celebrated by the first Christian community, and referred to them as the breaking of bread. We can imagine the great love with which Our Lady received her son.

Nobody can guide us like Mary in acquiring an appreciation of this mystery of faith. And we're told in the Ave Verum, "Behold the true body born of the Virgin Mary, who truly suffered and was sacrificed on the cross for men."

Here is the church's treasure, the heart of the world, our senses fail.

"Visus, tactus, gustus in te fallitur," as the words of the hymn, "Adoro te devote" tell us, which reminds us that faith alone, in the words of Christ, can lead us to the center of this Eucharistic mystery.

*I thank you, my God, for the good resolutions, affections, and inspirations that you have communicated to me during this meditation. I ask your help to put them into practice. My immaculate Mother, St. Joseph, my father and Lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me. In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.*

*EW*
