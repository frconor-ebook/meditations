By Fr. Conor Donnelly

*(Proofread)*

*In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen. My Lord and my God, I firmly believe that You are here, that You see me, that You hear me. I adore You with profound reverence. I ask Your pardon for my sins and the grace to make this time of prayer fruitful. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me.*

"He entered Jericho and was going through the town and suddenly, a man whose name was Zacchaeus made his appearance. He was one of the senior tax collectors and a wealthy man. He kept trying to see who Jesus was, but he was too short and could not see him for the crowd. So he ran ahead and climbed a sycamore tree to catch a glimpse of Jesus who was to pass that way" (Luke 19:1--4).

"And Jesus reached the spot. He looked up and spoke to him. 'Zacchaeus, come down. Hurry. I must stay at your house today.' And he hurried down and welcomed him joyfully" (Luke 19:5--6). We see the desire of Zacchaeus to meet Christ using all the means that were necessary. And in this passage of the gospel, we can focus our attention on God's infinite mercy. It's logical that the church should give so much emphasis to this great reality. What could be more important than the mercy of God?

It's a fountain of our hope. We creatures are very much in need of divine pardon. We do well to remind ourselves frequently that the Lord is full of mercy. There is a story that took place a long time ago in France during the years following the French Revolution. It's about an old man who walked with a limp. He had been a respected soldier in Napoleon's army, but battle wounds had ended his promising career. And he traveled from village to village begging, which he hated to do. He arrived in a village in Western France one evening and made his way to the steps of the church. He was willing to take coins from churchgoers, but he despised them, despised them all, all the same, for believing in a God who could have permitted the terrible crimes that he had witnessed.

The next morning, the priest found the beggar, huddled on the steps in a worn blanket and after Mass, invited him to come to his house for breakfast. The beggar felt repulsed as he always did when taking charity, but hunger and the kindness in the priest's face made him accept. The stranger stayed there for several days. The priest's kindness never waned. The beggar had never been loved so much, so selflessly. And he had never felt so miserable. Finally, the beggar asked for confession. And one of his sins stood out from all the rest. He had been the most trusted servant of an aristocratic family. The head of that family had unsuccessfully rebelled against the Revolution, and his wife and six children entrusted their lives and fortunes to this well-trusted servant. But for a pouch of gold coins, he betrayed his master's wife and children and watched them go one by one to the guillotine.

Only the youngest child somehow escaped and no one had seen him since. With tears of shame, the stranger finishes confession. The priest gave him absolution, raised him up and embraced him. As the beggar's eyes lifted, he saw a portrait on the wall behind, the portrait of the family he had betrayed to their deaths. Shocked, he pulled back from the priest. Who are you, he asked, and where did you get that painting? The priest smiled. I am the youngest son, my friend, and I forgive you. Thus, God's pardon reaches to everybody and to all sins. We are very much in need of that forgiveness. Our greatest treasure as Catholics is the revelation of Christ. A revelation of God's boundless mercy. The only force strong enough to penetrate the walls of pain, of anger, of fear and resentment that we have built around our hearts. Grace works through confession. It's a time for new beginnings. All things work out for the good. It's a great thing to make our peace with God, and to make our peace with others.

The Gospel tells us the story of the meeting between Jesus and Zacchaeus. The Lord was passing through Jericho on his way to Jerusalem. At the entrance of the city, he had cured a blind beggar from his affliction. The beggar had won his cure because of his faithful, persevering prayer to Jesus despite the admonitions of the crowd. And once Our Lord had entered the city, the streets were thronged with people and there, amidst the crowd, was one of the senior tax collectors, a wealthy man who was well known in Jericho. The Roman Empire had no officials of its own for the collection of taxes. Consequently, it used the local people for this purpose. They were free to engage agents. *The Navarre Bible* says the global amount of tax for each region was specified by the Roman authorities. The tax collectors levied more than this amount, keeping the surplus for themselves. And this led them to act rather arbitrarily, which was why the people hated them.

In the case of the Jews, insult was added to injury by the fact that the chosen people were being exploited by the Gentiles. Saint Luke tells us that Zacchaeus was anxious to see what kind of man Jesus was, "but he was too short and could not see him for the crowd" (Luke 19:3). Yet he eventually got his wish by putting to one side any concern he might have had for public opinion. So he ran on ahead and shinnied up a sycamore tree to catch a glimpse of Jesus who was to pass that way. He forgot his pride and his vanity. Zacchaeus must have had a burning desire in his heart for him to put his reputation at risk by this kind of behavior. It gives us a great example of all of us who might share his longing to see Jesus, to remain with him.

Let us take advantage of our prayer today to examine the sincerity and vigor of our desires. How much do we really want to be with Jesus? Pope Saint John Paul comments, do I want to see Christ? Do I do everything to see him? This question, he says, 2,000 years later, is as relevant as it was then when Jesus passed through the cities and villages of his land. It's a relevant question for each of us personally today. Do I want to? Do I really want to? Or do I perhaps rather avoid the encounter with him? Do I prefer not to see him? Or do I prefer him not to see me? At least in my way of thinking and feeling. And if I already see him in some way, then do I prefer to see him from afar, not drawing too near, a bit like Peter in the Garden of Gethsemane, followed Our Lord at a distance, venturing before his eyes so as not to perceive too much, so as not to have to accept the whole truth that is in him, that comes from him, from Christ.

And we know that Christ will reward our efforts to draw near to him. He will more than reward us. When Jesus reached the spot, he looked up and spoke to him. "Zacchaeus, come down. Hurry, for I must stay at your house today" (Luke 19:5). What great news. That man who was content to see Jesus from his perch on a tree had been called by his name. Jesus treated him as an old friend. With great naturalness, Jesus invites himself to the home of Zacchaeus. Saint Augustine wrote, Zacchaeus had given so much importance to seeing Jesus pass by that way, and so by that, he earned the pleasure of his company at home. The Master read his heart. He did not want to forgo this unique opportunity. And Zacchaeus discovers that he is a person, personally, personally loved by the one who introduced himself as the awaited Messiah. He's touched to the depth of his soul and opens his heart. Right away, he wants to be next to the Master. "He hurried down and welcomed him joyfully" (Luke 19:6).

And Pope Saint John Paul continues, he had found the immense joy which accompanies every true encounter with Jesus. Zacchaeus received the Master. Now he had everything. He was not frightened by the fact that to receive Christ into his house might jeopardize, for example, his professional career, or make some difficult actions connected with his activity as chief of tax collector difficult. He shows by his actions that he has genuinely embarked on a new life. He's become another disciple of the Master. "Look, sir, I give half my property to the poor. And if I have cheated anybody, I will pay him back four times the amount" (Luke 19:8). Zacchaeus promised to make restitution beyond what was required by the Mosaic law. In addition, he's giving away half of his fortune to the poor. So once we encounter Christ, he will inspire us to be generous with other people. He will move us to share whatever we have with the needy. Zacchaeus understood that his discipleship required a complete detachment from temporal goods.

We're told in *The Forge*, my God, I see, I shall never accept you as my savior unless I acknowledge you as my model at the same time. Since you yourself chose to be poor, make me love holy poverty. I resolve with your grace, to live and die in poverty, even though I may have millions at my disposal. So Christ is always seeking us out. He encourages us to have the virtue of hope. When Jesus went into the house of Zacchaeus, many people complained at his association with a sinner. They said, "He has gone to stay at a sinner's house" (Luke 19:7). But Zacchaeus, we're told, stood his ground and said to the Lord, "Lord, sir, I am going to give half my property to the poor, and if I have cheated anybody, I will pay him back four times the amount" (Luke 19:8). Jesus said to him, "Today salvation has come to this house because this man too is a son of Abraham. For the Son of Man has come to seek out and save what was lost" (Luke 19:9--10).

Our Lord responds to the conversion of Zacchaeus with some of the most beautiful words of the gospels. "Today, salvation has come to this house because this man too is a son of Abraham. For the Son of Man has come to seek out and save what was lost" (Luke 19:9--10). Our Lord's call is a call full of hope. If God ever permits us to experience difficulties, or times of trial, contradictions in our family, we must trust in the sure knowledge that the good shepherd will always be watchful on our behalf. Saint Ambrose has written, of all people to choose from, he singled out the chief of the tax collectors. Who can lose hope for themselves when even such a man attained salvation. So Our Lord never forgets his own. The figure of Zacchaeus tells us that no one is beyond the reach of God's grace. As far as the people of Jericho were concerned, Zacchaeus was an outcast from the chosen people. Nevertheless, from the time of his arrival in Jericho, Our Lord was thinking of that man. Sometimes we too have to think in our prayer of similar people in our life, in the periphery of our contacts, of our professional life, classmates from of old, primary school buddies, or friends. Try and stay in contact with people. Keep the contact warm. Because maybe we are the person that God wants to use to bring that person closer.

Despite all the appearances to the contrary, Zacchaeus had a great longing to see the Master. We don't know what's going on in the minds or hearts of those who may be appear to be far away from God. According to Saint Luke's account, he was also prepared to repent of his injustice and dishonesty, and to make reparation. It's a reminder to us every time we might meet with a dishonest official, or to try and leave some little trace, or prick that conscience, and give them a prayer card or some little thing. There are many people not unlike Zacchaeus in our environment. They would very much like to see Christ. They're waiting for someone to look upon them with compassion, and invite them to a new life. We should never abandon hope in anyone, even when all seems lost. God is omnipotent, and his mercy is limitless. It exceeds all human judgment. There's a story of a holy woman who had a remarkable experience of the power of divine mercy. One of her relatives had committed suicide by jumping off a bridge. And this woman was so distraught, and depressed by the event. But she was unable even to pray for her unfortunate kinsperson, her relative. One day, the Lord asked her why she did not pray for this relative as she did for other people. Somewhat taken aback by the words of Jesus, she answered, you know very well that he jumped off a bridge and killed himself. Our Lord responded saying, don't forget that between the bridge and the water, I was present. This woman had never doubted the divine mercy before, but from that day, her confidence in Our Lord knew no bounds. She prayed from then on for her poor relative with renewed intensity and faith. So the moral of these accounts is the same. Whenever we consider the goodness and compassion of God for his sons and daughters, we will be cured of our earthbound pessimism. Lord, may I never doubt your goodness and mercy that you have for your children. No matter how extreme the circumstances may be. Let me firmly resolve to bring people to you, because your mercy will be greater than all our poor human limitations. "His mercy is from generation to generation, on those who fear him" (Luke 1:50). God's mercy is infinite. It's greater than his justice. God is the Father of Mercy. And we see this manifested in many places, in many ways. Also in Our Lord's dealings with Zacchaeus. Through these stories, God wants to reveal the mystery of his love. And we also see, as John Paul the Second said, how modern man needs mercy. One of the principal themes of Our Lord's preaching was mercy in fresh aspects all the time. The story of the Good Shepherd, the parable of the Lost Coin. Luke's Gospel, in particular, is the gospel of mercy. And Christ, in his person, reveals the content of the mercy of God. And highlights to us how our lives should be guided by mercy and love. Christ is the model of mercy towards others. We're told in the Beatitudes that the merciful shall obtain mercy. Mercy signifies a special power of love that prevails over sin. The subtleties of love become manifest in God's mercy. Mercy is a notion that pertains to God, but also to men. It's one of the signs of the closeness of man to God. In contrast to it, with God's justice, mercy is shown to be more powerful, more profound. Thus, we find mercy mentioned in all sorts of ways in the gospel. Justice ends up meaning salvation won by God, that is mercy. It excludes all sorts of hatred towards others. So mercy is love's second name. On the cross, we find the revelation of mercy. "God so loved the world that he gave his only begotten Son" (John 3:16). "Father, forgive them for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). The beatitude, "Blessed are the Merciful," is the synthesis, the synthesis of the whole of the good news. "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy" (Matt. 5:7). And human hearts are capable of mercy. Animals are not. "Those sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven. Those sins you shall retain, they are retained" (John 20:23). So Christ is the inexhaustible, inexhaustible source of mercy. There's a mystery of mercy that plays out in the passages of the gospel. The real meaning of mercy in the parable of the Prodigal Son is manifested when the son is restored to value. All the evil is overcome with good. We're told in the Magnificat that "his mercy is from generation to generation, on those who fear him" (Luke 1:50). Christ deserves mercy from those to whom he did good. But he did not receive it. In all sorts of ways, he gives us an example of the breathtaking mercy of God. It's almost beyond our comprehension. Christ gave his life, and by this, is measured his love. Mercy is a constant in God's attitude towards mankind. He seeks out the individuals. "Zacchaeus, come down, I must stay at your house today" (Luke 19:5). So, in the scriptures and in the liturgy, we say, "Show us, O Lord, your mercy, and grant us your salvation" (Ps. 85:7). Our Lord tells us that the condition for obtaining compassion ourselves, and mercy, is that we have a heart of compassion for those around us. In the conclusion of a document called *Veritatis Splendor*, the Splendor of Truth, Saint John Paul the Second likes to talk about Our Lady as the mother of mercy. She has this title, he says, because her son, Jesus Christ, was sent by the Father as the revelation of God's mercy. Thus, Mary magnifies God's mercy. Mercy then, or Mary then, rather, is the one, he says, who has the deepest knowledge of the mystery of God's mercy. She knows its price. She knows how great it is. So we can turn always to the mother of mercy, our sweetness and our hope, knowing that we will always find mercy, compassion, understanding from that mother, and find our home in the heart of Our Lady.

*I thank You, my God, for the good resolutions, affections, and inspirations that You have communicated to me during this meditation. I ask Your help to put them into practice. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me. In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.*
