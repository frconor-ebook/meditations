By Fr. Conor Donnelly

*(Proofread)*

*In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen. My Lord and my God, I firmly believe that You are here, that You see me, that You hear me. I adore You with profound reverence. I ask Your pardon for my sins and grace to make this time of prayer fruitful. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me.*

We're told in the account of Our Lord's passion, towards the end, it says, "They will look on him whom they have pierced" (John 19:37). Well, Benedict says those words are like a model for the whole of humanity, to contemplate the wounds of Christ.

Sometimes dialogue with people means just looking at each other. This is what happens with people in love. And love of God and dialogue with Our Lord can be just like that. A look can be enough. A peaceful look, a look that doesn't involve our bodily eyes.

When a Christian prays, he realizes with greater or lesser clarity that God completely transcends the conceptual realm. At least at times he detects, perhaps more with his heart than with his intellect, that he is unable to express all that he knows about God and his ways with words. These are the experience Saint Josemaria manifested at times, even in front of large groups of people.

In speaking of God, he says, "How great you are and how beautiful and how good. How silly I am for trying to understand you. How little would you be if you fitted inside my head. But you fit in my heart, which is no small matter."

An experience of this kind is a gift from God, with different levels of depth. And we usually call it contemplation.

In spiritual theology, the term is reserved for a type of prayer in which the insufficiency of words becomes evident, due to an intense and profound love granted by God's spirit, and thanks to which a very simple knowledge of God is attained.

There is continuity between prayer and contemplation. Just as a river branches out, thereby forming its delta before it flows into the sea, so too, the flow of prayer first branches out into words, be they external or merely internal, until it reaches its goal in the ocean of contemplation.

Saint Josemaria says, "First one aspiration, then another and another, until our fervor seems insufficient, because words are too poor. And one gives way to intimacy with God, by looking at God without rest and without fatigue."

In ancient philosophy, the term contemplation signified an elevated and simple way of knowing the truth. Rational knowledge proceeds by way of a discursive process. Beginning with what is perceived through the senses, by means of abstraction, concepts are then formed and judgment is possible. But when this process is simplified, a certain contemplation of what is known can be reached. Saint Thomas Aquinas calls this a simple intuition of the truth, or a clear and simple look of the truth.

Christian contemplation is of another order, because of the role that love plays in it. Saint John states, "He who does not love does not know God; for God is love" (1 John 4:8). It's true that in philosophical contemplation love for truth and joy in the perception of beauty are present. But not in the same way as in Christian contemplation.

The latter is a gift from God, which consists in simple knowledge which derives from supernatural love, and leads to knowing God and his designs for salvation in a simple and profound way, as well as to their enjoyment.

Christian contemplation is not just activity of the intellect, but rather belongs to the will, which moves all of the faculties to their acts, including the intellect. That's why Saint Gregory puts contemplative life in love for God, since through love, one is inflamed to contemplate God's beauty. And since every man finds delight when he has attained what he loves, it follows that the contemplative life terminates in delight.

As far as the term contemplation is concerned, it's enough to point out that it has its root in *contemplum*, a word that designates the platform situated in front of some pagan temples, from which one looks up at the heavens. Hence the word *contemplare*, to look far off. This Latin term translates the Greek *theoria*, which comes from *thea*, meaning a vision.

*Theoria* in Greek means to look or to watch as if watching a spectacle, fixing one's attention on what one is seeing. That is, looking with interest on something that is worth the effort because of its aesthetic or artistic value, such as a landscape or a sculpture. In philosophy, the term is used to refer to contemplating the truth. Knowledge of the first principles is of this type, both those of the intellect of speculative order and those of the moral or practical order.

Saint Thomas also points out that contemplation is an act of the cognitive power directed by the will. Combining all these elements, according to his doctrine, contemplation can be defined as a simple and non-discursive knowledge of the supreme truth that proceeds from love. And to this we can add that beauty in and of itself and by its very essence is found in contemplation. Beauty is defined as that the very knowledge of which pleases us.

In the same line of thought, Saint John of the Cross describes contemplation as the science of love, an infused and loving knowledge of God which enlightens the soul and at the same time enkindles it with love, until it is raised up step by step, even unto God its creator. For it is love alone that unites and joins the soul with God.

Contemplation is not only intellectual knowledge, but personal communion with the one and triune God. Contemplation does not take place by rising above the material and the temporal, since God created the universe and called man to communion with him in the temporal sphere. Rather, contemplation takes place through openness to the word, by which God makes himself known, and through openness to the fact that he is dwelling in the interiority of our soul, although in a way that transcends it at the same time.

Contemplation is not introspection, but rather knowledge and love of God. It is to know loving and to love knowing, which implies going out of oneself and giving oneself to the one in whom lies the fullness of one's own being.

As a loving knowledge of God, contemplation entails loving what God loves, such as love of others and love for the world expressed in deeds. Saint Josemaria speaks of contemplation, sometimes without explaining what he means by the term. He's simply using the word in its traditional sense, which would have been familiar to his listeners. He speaks of the same contemplation that God has granted to many saints who are called contemplatives. At the same time, he broadens the scope of the concept to new heights. He refers to contemplation of the middle of the world and to be contemplatives in ordinary life, expressions that occur throughout his preaching and writings. He affirms that the entire life of a Christian can be a constant contemplation of God in and through one's ordinary tasks. Contemplation is not something that takes place in particular moments, but it can be present as the ultimate end of the Christian life in everything one does. This is the best way to give glory to God in the present life, and the most perfect anticipation of the face-to-face vision of God in the life to come.

The expressions Saint Josemaria employs indicate that he understands contemplation as being in continuity with the Christian tradition, advancing along the same spiritual route. It is a gift which consists in the loving knowledge of God that takes place as a simple gaze. It is an activity that does not involve the discursive process of reason. For instance, he writes that in contemplation, "one does not reason, one gazes." As is true with Saint Teresa of Avila, the verb to gaze is a key term, suggesting that this gift is a particular anticipation here on earth of the face-to-face vision of God in heaven.

Being a gaze, contemplation is a simple form of knowledge of God, which cannot be translated into words, for the tongue cannot express itself adequately. With this, the intellect grows calm, as is characteristic when perceiving the beauty of the mystery of God reflected in nature or in human work. It is the look of love that resembles the mother's look for her child. It is in looking at God as one looks at a father, as one looks at a friend, as one whom one loves madly.

Contemplation is a look towards divine love, as Saint Josemaria, as the most Holy Virgin did. It is a gaze at the pure light of love with which we see God in all earthly realities, in people, in events, in what is great, and in what seems small, in what pleases us, and in what we find painful. Contemplation, says Saint Josemaria, is discovering that divine something which is found in the details. It is a love by which we come to know God and to hope for a full union with him. All the theological virtues are actively exercised in the contemplative life of a child of God. It is a loving contemplation that brings joy to the soul, because where there is love, everything is happiness.

"I'm not talking about extraordinary situations," as Saint Josemaria. "These are, they may very well be ordinary happenings within our soul." He didn't wish to say that these are ordinary phenomena of extraordinary persons, but rather they are ordinary phenomena of any ordinary Christian. Contemplation is not a gift reserved only to exceptional souls. It is God's kindness which is part of the ordinary path of holiness. Saint Josemaria preaches that everyone can be a contemplative. And in so doing, he's by no means the first one in the Christian tradition to do so. To cite just one example, a cardinal, a French cardinal in the 17th century, he writes, "Christians are dedicated and called by vocation to contemplation as something essential. We are called to contemplation not simply by inspiration, but by the state and the condition of life and grace they received at baptism."

One could say that contemplation begins each time the Christian realizes that in raising his heart to God in prayer and calling him Father, and in opening himself up to God's love and trusting in his power, goodness, mercy, and wisdom, God's glory infinitely surpasses these terms. In other words, the Christian sees God with the light of a living faith and savors him, even though he's unable to explain what he sees or tastes. This more or less clear perception of the insufficiency of human words in the relationship with God, which is already a certain budding of contemplation, pertains to the essence of prayer. On the contrary, whoever imagines that when praying he encompasses God with his words, and is not conscious that God surpasses him absolutely, would not be praying at all. His prayer would not be adoration.

The seed of contemplation, which any believer in the state of grace can experience when praying devoutly, is initially weak and nearly imperceptible. Nonetheless, it is a true beginning of contemplation, because the living faith put into practice in prayer is the first manifestation of what we will one day contemplate in its fullness. "By the light of faith," says Saint Josemaria, "we enter into the divine truth." It is a seed that is destined to grow by the action of the Holy Spirit, and can reach, with each one's personal cooperation, the highest peaks of what is properly called contemplative prayer.

Personal correspondence to this gift demands struggle, ascetical struggle, that purifies the soul from sin and its consequences. But this struggle is not just a mere step before reaching the mystical union with God that contemplation entails. On the contrary, contemplation already takes place in the very struggle against that which opposes itself to the love of God. This, however, is not a victory based on human strength. Rather, it is always a gift of which the Christian, aided by grace, makes himself capable of receiving through struggle. Saint Josemaria does not use the classical distinction between acquired contemplation and infused contemplation to refer to this divine and human cooperative venture. After having described contemplative prayer, whereby, he says, "one does not reason, one gazes, and the soul feels and knows it is under the loving gaze of God," he comments, "Ascetism, mysticism, I don't mind what you call it. Whatever it is, ascetism or mysticism, doesn't matter. Either way, it's a gift of God's mercy." This is the way many Christians should live, each one forging ahead along his own spiritual path. There are countless in the midst of the cares of the world.

We're told by Saint Paul in his first letter to Timothy that "God desires all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Tim. 2:4). He calls everyone to know him and to enter into a relationship with him here on earth, and to see him face-to-face in heaven. Contemplation, he says, is nothing other than the highest and deepest form of this type of relationship, a foretaste of heaven, the most perfect beginning of the beatific vision. And just as all can and should have a relationship with God, all are called to contemplate God.

Contemplation is a knowledge of God that is founded on the harmony created by love. Saint Thomas says the one who loves identifies in some manner with the one loved. Love unites those who love one another, establishing a connaturality between them. And that connatural relation is the foundation for a deep knowledge. He who loves intensely often perceives what is in the interior of the person loved without needing to make use of the process of reason. A look can be sufficient to understand and communicate many things without the need of any explanation. This communication gives rise to trust, which facilitates mutual self-giving. "People in love," says Saint Josemaria, "surrender themselves confidently. Their hearts beat wonderfully in unison with the single love. What then would the love of God be like?"

Naturally, when it comes to divine contemplation, we are dealing with a reality that lies well beyond that which exists between two human beings, regardless of how closely connected the two may be, since the love of God, our supernatural charity, is the very participation in trinitarian love. The Christian who is divinized by grace contemplates God from within and not from without. He contemplates God by participating in the divine life, as the Psalm suggests: "For with you is the fountain of life; in your light do we see light" (Ps. 36:9). In contrast to a person who is loved by another creature, where the presence of the beloved in the one who loves is only intentional, the Trinity really dwells in the soul of the Christian who loves God.

Even the weakest charity allows for connatural knowledge of God. Such is in the case of someone who limits himself to avoid falling into grave sin, but who does not attempt to fulfill God's will in everything. However, for as long as this attitude is maintained, it will only give rise to a passing knowledge. "If anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer," we are told in the letter of Saint James, "he is like a man who observes his natural face in a mirror; for he observes himself and goes away and at once forgets what he was like" (James 1:23--24). Things are very different with the one who, with the impulse of grace, freely tries to identify his will to the will of God by removing any obstacles to the action of the Holy Spirit. Thus his connaturality with God grows, because the Holy Spirit pours into his soul the gift of wisdom along with charity and all the other gifts, understanding, piety, and knowledge, that make him even more deeply acquainted with God. "As time passes," says Saint Josemaria, "with increasing frequency, and corresponding to grace, words are not needed, because the tongue cannot express itself. The intellect grows calm. One does not reason, one gazes, and the soul feels and knows it is under the loving gaze of God all day long."

So contemplation is a loving knowledge of God based on connaturality with God, which arises from supernatural charity with faith and hope and the gifts of the Holy Spirit. Connaturality with God through love has its foundation in the Christian's participation in the divine nature as God's adopted child through the sending of the Holy Spirit. But it is also founded on the incarnation of the only begotten Son of God, who made himself connatural to man by assuming a human nature. By allowing us to see God made man on earth, Christ has strengthened the connaturality of the human creature with God, thereby facilitating contemplation. Thus, the letter of Saint John says to us, "That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life, ... we proclaim also to you, so that you may have fellowship with us; and our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ" (1 John 1:1--3).

The apostles and first disciples and Saint Josemaria were not the only ones who had the possibility of contemplating God through Jesus Christ. In every age, the Christian can contemplate the life of Jesus, the passage of God on our earth, the marvel of a God who loves with the human heart. It is now also possible to come intimately close to Jesus in body and soul. Christ has pointed out the way to us clearly, through the bread and through the word: nourishing ourselves with the Eucharist, and knowing and fulfilling what he came to teach us, as we confess with him in prayer.

In addition to allowing the Christian to contemplate God through the humanity of Christ, the incarnation of the divine Word facilitates contemplation through created realities and noble human activities, given that, in having been assumed by the Word, their most profound meaning has been revealed. Saint Paul says to the Colossians, "All things were created through him and for him" (Col. 1:16).

We can also consider the fact that a Christian lives the same supernatural life of Christ. So when he contemplates God, he does so united with Christ through the Holy Spirit. Contemplation is knowing and loving with Christ and in Christ, knowing and loving with the mind and heart of Christ. The Apostle expresses this in a passage that contains the whole program of contemplative life: knowledge and love, prayer and life. Saint Paul says to the Ephesians, "That Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith; that you, being rooted and grounded in love, may have power to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth and length and height and depth, and to know the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled with all the fullness of God" (Eph. 3:17--19).

A few weeks before his passage to heaven, Saint Josemaria summarized his understanding of the contemplative life, which he ardently sought at the time. "May I see with your eyes," he said, "my Christ, Jesus of my soul." Just as the Christian gives glory to God through Jesus Christ, through him, with him, and in him, so contemplation is equally through Christ, with Christ, and in Christ. Saint Josemaria teaches us to ask for the gift of this contemplative life, and to seek it with earnestness in every action, in ongoing simple and filial union with God our Father.

Contemplation can take place in the ordinary life of a child of God, not only at times dedicated exclusively to prayer. Saint Josemaria persistently preached that Christians can be contemplative souls, in constant dialogue, conversing with God continually, from the first thought in the morning to the last thought of night. "We, children of God," he said, "ought to be contemplatives, people who in the midst of the din of the throng know how to find silence of soul in a lasting conversation with the Lord. People who know how to look at him as they look at a father, as they look at a friend, as they look at someone with whom they are madly in love."

We can ask Our Lady that we might learn from her contemplative spirit, to grow in that spirit of contemplation in a regular way every day.

*I thank You, my God, for the good resolutions, affections, and inspirations that You have communicated to me during this meditation. I ask Your help to put them into practice. My Immaculate Mother, Saint Joseph, my father and lord, my guardian angel, intercede for me. In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.*
